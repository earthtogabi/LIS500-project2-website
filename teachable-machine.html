Training Notes

  • Images were captured in all four corners of the screen + center.

    • one → 503 images
    • two → 578 images
    • three → 467 images

📌 Project Reflection Statement

Below is our full project reflection. The text is placed inside a scrollable box to keep the webpage clean and easy to navigate.

As we read Unmasking AI, it would be insufficient to say that we only learned a lesson or two when in reality our eyes were opened to many implicit biases and gaps within the AI boom. As we worked to complete this final project, one of the main lessons that Dr. Joy Buolamwini specifically emphasized in her book, Unmasking AI, was that AI models have a “coded gaze.” Because AI models are trained with data and created by human coders, it is impossible to prevent implicit bias from entering the system. For example, if the data being used does not take into account diversity or intersectionality, it is extremely likely that one would create a biased and unfair system. Additionally, humans have many types of implicit biases that they themselves may not even notice, making it easier for this bias to creep into their work. Although this concept might seem intuitive, the world of AI often obscures the bias embedded in its results.

Buolamwini witnessed firsthand these biased systems with her “Mirror of Positivity.” Her mirror was designed to show positive affirmations around her reflection, but the code she used failed to recognize her face. At first this seemed trivial, but it opened her eyes to how biased the code was—and how quickly such bias can ruin the user experience. She later discovered that many AI systems created by highly competitive companies operate using biased datasets and produce biased outcomes. Despite the fact that these companies took steps to avoid bias, eliminating it is nearly impossible because of the implicit biases embedded in the human mind. Buolamwini emphasizes the importance of testing systems for bias, because bias cannot always be seen with the naked eye. As we developed our own Teachable Machine, we kept her message in mind and tested our model to reduce bias as much as possible.

As we tried not only to incorporate the least amount of bias but also to test our machine for hidden bias, Buolamwini’s lessons on intersectionality also resonated with us. Intersectionality is the idea that different aspects of social identity interact to give individuals unique experiences and privileges. Although this concept originally felt overwhelming when introduced at the beginning of the course, we gradually realized how integral it is to understanding human experience. When Buolamwini discussed AI systems and their effects on people, intersectionality consistently played a significant role. Because developing AI requires specialized knowledge, it is often created by a small, homogeneous group of people—which can limit diversity in perspectives. Since individuals with similar identities tend to share similar implicit biases, those biases may influence the development of AI without being noticed. For instance, when Buolamwini created her positivity mirror, the original authors of the code had included only white faces in their dataset, which likely caused no problems during testing because the creators themselves were probably white. Understanding the role of intersectionality in perpetuating bias, we made sure that our group reviewed our model collectively and included different types of data whenever possible.

Having a diverse set of data is important for reducing bias, but during our Teachable Machine process, we discovered that diverse data can also introduce complexity and errors. Our model was built to detect how many fingers the user was holding up to the camera, but the more images we uploaded, the more misclassifications appeared. We eventually realized that depending on the direction the hand was facing, the model sometimes interpreted a different number of fingers. To solve this, we restricted our dataset to images where the user’s hand faced directly toward the camera. This improved accuracy, but it also showed us that creating a robust model requires carefully choosing which images to include—and that excluding images is sometimes necessary.

Based on these takeaways, it may seem that AI and machine learning bring more harm than good to society. But although Buolamwini points out their dangers, she also emphasizes that these technologies are created by people who genuinely hope to improve the world. In order to make something better, we must first identify its flaws. Acknowledging limits and drawbacks does not mean abandoning AI altogether; rather, it means understanding where improvements are needed. Even with our best efforts, our Teachable Machine may still contain bias or limitations. But that does not make our model inherently harmful—what matters is our willingness to acknowledge these issues and continue improving.

Finally, one of the most powerful lessons we gained from Buolamwini and other authors in this course is the importance of continuing to work toward positive change. Although it may feel overwhelming to confront the many problems—such as implicit bias—around us, identifying these issues is the first essential step. Many writers we studied highlighted the harms that technology, including hashtags and AI, has brought into society. But by acknowledging these harms, we can begin working toward solutions. Coding is inherently challenging, and thinking about its social implications can make it even more intimidating. Buolamwini and others remind us that perfection is not the goal—growth is. As we complete this assignment, we are not striving for a flawless product, but rather to learn from our mistakes so that we can become better coders and create more positive technological impacts in the future.
