Teachable Machine Project

This page showcases our group’s machine learning experiment using Google’s Teachable Machine. We trained a hand sign classifier and connected it to a p5.js sketch so that the model can recognize numbers using live webcam input.

Live ML Demo

Use the webcam demo below to try our hand sign classifier. Hold up your right hand, palm facing the camera, and show the hand signs for one, two, or three.

Model Training Process

Our model classifies three hand signs: one, two, and three. We collected separate image datasets for each class using Teachable Machine’s webcam capture tool.

  • Initially planned to classify hand signs 1–5, but reduced to 1–3 due to the large number of photos needed.
  • We restarted the dataset after noticing that rotating the hand caused all fingers to line up, which confused the model.
  • The final dataset uses only the right hand, with the palm facing forward.
  • Images were captured with the hand in all corners of the frame as well as the center, to improve robustness.
  • Approximate image counts per class: one ≈ 503, two ≈ 578, three ≈ 467.

Model Behavior and Results

Overall, the classifier performs well when the hand is clearly visible, close to the camera, and matches the training conditions (right hand, palm facing forward). However, we noticed several limitations that connect directly to issues discussed in Unmasking AI:

  • Performance drops when lighting changes or the background is cluttered.
  • The model becomes less confident when the hand is partially out of frame.
  • Left-hand signs or rotated hands are often misclassified, because they were not included in the training data.
  • Different skin tones, camera quality, or distance from the camera could also affect accuracy, even though our small dataset doesn’t fully represent that diversity.

Ethical Reflection & Connections to Unmasking AI

As a group, we used this small hand sign classifier to think about the much larger questions Joy Buolamwini raises in Unmasking AI — especially how biased datasets and narrow definitions of “normal” bodies can lead to unfair outcomes. Our model “works” only for a specific set of conditions: one right hand, palm facing forward, in front of a webcam. This mirrors how real-world systems often silently exclude people whose bodies, gestures, or environments do not match the training data.
